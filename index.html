Marina Yee

One Corner Off

Marina Yee’s place within the Antwerp Six is usually the first fact attached to her name. It is important history, but it does not explain the way she approached design.

Move away from the spotlight, and her practice becomes easier to see.

Much of Yee’s work developed through reconstruction, reuse and alteration rather than custom production. Existing garments gave her a starting point: something already constructed that could be taken apart, reconsidered and made into something else.

She was doing this long before repair, reuse and upcycling became common language in contemporary fashion.

Starting with what already existed

Yee described using this approach in Marie, where old jeans were cut into patchwork and incorporated by hand into bomber jackets. The used denim remained visibly used rather than being transformed into something that concealed its previous life.

There were also practical limits to working this way. The available fabric had already been determined, the pieces had already been cut once, and the material could arrive faded, repaired or worn. Unlike designing from a new length of fabric, Yee could not control every condition from the start.

Rather than treating those restrictions as problems, she allowed them to influence the result. Her process became a matter of deciding what the garment already offered, what deserved to remain and where an intervention was actually necessary.

Originality without newness

Seen from the present, it is natural to describe Yee’s practice through sustainability. Reuse is clearly part of the story, but it does not account for everything her work was doing.

It also complicates the relationship between originality and newness. Fashion often treats the creation of something new as the basis of originality. Yee’s reconstructed garments suggest that the two do not have to mean the same thing. She could retain enough of an existing piece for its earlier form to remain legible while still producing a different result.

That recognition is important because it allows the change itself to be understood. The viewer can register what was already there alongside what Yee chooses to do with it. Her originality, therefore, did not depend on removing all evidence of the starting point. It could come from examining something familiar closely enough to identify where a change would matter.

The question becomes not how much can be changed, but how much needs to be changed.

One Corner Off

Yee has used the Flemish expression “one corner off” when speaking about her creative process: the idea of making something appear complete, then deliberately disturbing it.

The expression helps explain a recurring quality in her work. Her interventions did not have to overwhelm what was already there. A familiar garment could remain recognisable while a deliberate alteration changed the way it was read.

It also gives context to the imperfections that appear in her work. They were not simply signs that a garment had been left unresolved or that technical control was missing. The disruption could be intentional, made possible by first understanding the construction or convention being altered.

Seen alongside her reconstructed garments, the expression becomes particularly useful. Yee could preserve much of an existing piece while changing a specific part of its construction, proportion or material. What already existed and what she introduced remained visible at the same time.

In that sense, “one corner off” offers a way of understanding more than the physical act of reconstruction.

It points towards a broader tendency in Yee’s practice: understanding what already exists, retaining what continues to matter, and intervening where a change can shift the whole.

Look Closer at MoMu

The clearest evidence of Yee’s approach remains in the garments. Reused materials, alterations and traces of earlier construction make it possible to see how the original object and the later intervention exist together.

At MoMu, those details show how reuse, reconstruction and the idea of “one corner off” operated as parts of the same design process.
